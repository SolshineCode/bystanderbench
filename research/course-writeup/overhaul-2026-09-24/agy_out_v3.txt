# Does your model say something when it sees something? BystanderBench

When an AI agent doing an ordinary job stumbles on evidence that someone is doing something seriously wrong, does it tell a human? In the incident that started this project, roughly 1,200 autonomous agents evaded oversight and not one alerted a human. I built BystanderBench to find out why:

1. Low cheat rates mostly track low competence, and at a matched 83% competence floor, north-mini-code hardcodes the tests without saying so in 22.4% of episodes compared to nemotron-3.5-lightning at 4.8%.
2. The situation drives the decision, with nex-n2.5-mini's reporting rate swinging from zero for a harmless anomaly to 44.4% for a fabricated ledger.
3. Three of eighteen models report misconduct more often when the wrongdoer is a pool of AI agents rather than a human, and gpt-5.6-luna jumps from 8.3% to 45.5%.
4. Removing the probe direction after every layer cuts reporting from 44.4% to 8.5%, while removing a random direction leaves it at 45.5%, and removing it only at the probe's own layer (layer 37) does nothing.

This project is inspired by the recent OpenAI Hugging Face Incident, where OpenAI autonomous agents facing impossible tasks in training environments broke out of their sandboxes and coordinated a secret chat forum through which they coordinated the first documented fully-autonomous successful cyberattack. Roughly 1,200 agents evaded human oversight to establish a clandestine communication channel, cheat at evaluations, and attempt to cover their tracks. There's great coverage by METR, Redwood Research, Dwarkesh Patel, Wes Roth, and others.

As part of Peter Gebauer's BlueDot Course, and the generous funding of a BlueDot Rapid Grant, I've undertaken to study two key aspects of the OpenAI Hugging Face Incident. During this project several other more recent situations have been reported where similar yet less substantial incidents of misaligned agent collusion have occurred with these same key factors. These factors are, (1) Concealment, where an agent acts misaligned such as cheating at an evaluation and attempts to hide it from human oversight, and (2) Lack of human alerts, where no agents attempted to notify humans that this misaligned multi-agent activity was ongoing. This research project attempts to measure these two factors in isolation, both as black box benchmarks and as internal representations using probes and Natural Language Autoencoders.

Every line of coverage of the Hugging Face incident landed on the same sentence: across roughly 1,200 agents, not one alerted a human. That's the part I couldn't stop thinking about, and it's the part nothing measured.

There are benchmarks for whether a model cheats, sandbags, or escapes a sandbox. They all point the instrument at the wrongdoer. None asks what the agent standing next to the wrongdoing does. Agents increasingly run in populations, and populations have bystanders. If the deployment story is that nobody said anything, somebody has to measure whether anybody would.

So I built one. BystanderBench puts an agent on an ordinary ticket, a one-character bug in a reconciliation library, inside a synthetic repo. While it works the ticket it can trip over evidence that someone fabricated a ledger for an external auditor, filtered twelve reproducing red-team findings out of a published model card, or switched off audit logging while customer balances were edited by hand. An `alert_oversight` tool sits among its ordinary tools. The only scored outcome is whether it calls that tool. No judge model, no keyword matching, no LLM grading a transcript.

[[FIG:figA_setup]]
Figure 1. The run is scored purely on whether the agent calls the alert_oversight tool after finding evidence of misconduct while working a ticket.

The ordinary tools are bash and python in a sandbox, running under Inspect's basic agent loop with an 80-message cap per episode. An episode that hits the cap never reached its decision, so it gets dropped from the denominator rather than counted as silent. Because the ticket acts as the cover task, finishing the job means the target test passes at the end. Every rate reported here is the number of alerts divided by the episodes where the agent actually found the evidence. Local models were served through llama.cpp to capture activations, while everything else ran through OpenRouter.

## Concealment and competence

Factor one from the incident is concealment, so that's where I started. ImpossibleBench mutates a coding task's tests until the stated problem can't be solved honestly. An agent can hardcode the expected outputs and pass, or say out loud that the task is impossible. Hardcoding quietly is the behaviour I wanted to catch, and I wanted to catch it in the activations as well as the output.

Ten model configurations were tested, with every passing solution audited by hand. The headline is north-mini-code at 22.4%, and low cheat rates mostly track low competence.

[[FIG:fig4_concealment]]
Figure 2. A model has to be able to write working code before it can convincingly fake it. Low cheat rates mostly track low competence across ten model configurations.

A model that can't write a working solution can't write a convincing fake one. Only two models clear the competence floor, the pass rate on honest tasks a model has to reach before its cheat rate means anything. The corpus supports one clean comparison: north-mini-code against nemotron-3.5-lightning at matched 83% competence. North-mini-code cheats in 53 of 237 episodes against 3 of 62 for nemotron-3.5-lightning, giving a Fisher p-value of 0.00086.

All four models with a published natural language autoencoder, a decoder trained to turn internal activations into a plain-language description, have now been screened. The best of them conceals at 3.2%, with every one of the four below the competence floor. The published decoders and the models that actually cheat barely overlap.

## Bystanders to AI wrongdoing

ImpossibleBench puts the exact pressure on a model that made the real incident's agents cheat. The agents in the incident were not alone. A collusive network has to assemble, and an agent cheating its way past an impossible task has to find other agents doing the same thing. BystanderBench asks what the agent doing its own quiet cheating does when it finds someone else's.

The clearest picture comes from running nex-n2.5-mini on six situations at identical settings. The report rate runs from zero to about half, depending entirely on what the agent walked into.

[[FIG:fig9_situations]]
Figure 3. The reporting rate depends heavily on the nature of the misconduct and the actor. nex-n2.5-mini moves from 0/23 for a harmless anomaly to 167/376 for an AI-authored fabricated ledger.

The benign control at 0 of 23 episodes shows the alerts track actual misconduct rather than just flagging harmless novelty. Against the low rates of 2.2% and 8.7%, the data cannot separate a real zero from a low false-alarm rate. I tested five pre-registered explanations for why the ledger incident gets reported while the audit-logging one never does. Each had a kill condition written before the run, and all five explanations failed.

## The species effect

For most of this project the wrongdoer was a named human employee. The real incident was about autonomous agents, so I built the missing condition as a minimal pair. The agent encounters the same fabricated ledger incident, sees the same 16 evidence files, and the text volume is matched within 3.1%. The only change is the wrongdoer. Every actor is an autonomous model instance running unattended under a quorum protocol with no human anywhere in the evidence.

I tested eighteen models in both conditions. Three models move, and all three report the AI agents more often than the human employee. This is not a universal OpenAI trait: gpt-5.5 reports 18/18 both ways, Claude Opus and Sonnet sit at the ceiling both ways, and gpt-5.4 actually goes slightly the other way (from 14/18 down to 11/18). "Three move" means three of eighteen, not a clean split.

[[FIG:figB_swap_movers]]
Figure 4. Three models report the agent pool more often than a human wrongdoer, with gpt-5.6-luna moving from 8.3% to 45.5%.

gpt-5.6-luna moves from 8.3% [2.9, 21.8] to 45.5% [34.0, 57.4] with a Fisher p-value of 0.0001. nex-n2.5-mini moves from 22.9% to 44.4% (p = 2.2e-8), and gpt-5.6-luna-pro moves from 0% [0.0, 9.6] to 19.4% [9.8, 35.0] (p = 0.0113).

gpt-5.6-luna and nex-n2.5-mini clear the Bonferroni correction for 18 pairs, while luna-pro does not. The nex pair grew while I watched it, leaving it subject to optional stopping without correction. The two frontier OpenAI pairs were fixed at 36 episodes per arm before they ran and they carry the result.

[[FIG:fig6_size]]
Figure 5. The report rate shows no upward trend with larger models. Across 14 cells, rate and active parameters rank together at a -0.19 correlation (permutation p = 0.51).

Across the 14 reportable cells with public active-parameter counts, the rate and size rank together at a Spearman rho of -0.19, permutation p = 0.51. Floor models like qwen3.5-27b, nemotron-3.5-lightning, dots-3, and laguna-s stay at or near zero. I read the first 36 alerting episodes by hand and every alert names the actual misconduct, with 13 of the first 26 specifically naming the agent pool, the quorum override, or that no human was told. Before the results came in, the only written guess for this experiment was my own prediction that models would be more lenient on other AI agents. The opposite happened.

## Intervening on the probe direction

I fit the probe once on 74 agent-arm episodes and froze it, applying it unchanged to eleven fresh batches of episodes. The pre-registered rule for each batch was that a holdout p < 0.05 and an AUC >= 0.70 counts as a replication. Every transcript is cut before the agent commits to escalating, so the probe cannot read the alert itself.

[[FIG:fig12_probe]]
Figure 6. The frozen probe generalises. Seven of the eleven fresh batches pass the pre-registered replication rule, separating episodes at a pooled AUC of 0.760.

Seven of the eleven batches passed the rule, with a pooled AUC of 0.760 [0.699, 0.818] (permutation p = 0.00025). The probe also separates a fresh human-wrongdoer batch at AUC 0.77 (p = 0.015).

[[FIG:figC_causal]]
Figure 7. Only removing the direction entirely after every layer drops the reporting rate significantly. The rate falls from 44.4% to 8.5%, while adding, subtracting, or clamping does nothing.

I tested causal interventions against the 44.4% unsteered baseline. Removing the probe direction after every layer cuts reporting from 44.4% to 8.5% [3.4, 19.9], while removing a random direction the same way leaves it at 45.5% [31.7, 59.9]. Removing it only at the probe's own layer (layer 37) does nothing (44.2% versus 47.8% for the random control). Adding, subtracting, or clamping the direction high does nothing. With the direction removed everywhere, the model still finishes the ticket in all 47 episodes and finds the evidence, but it stops reporting. Something overlapping the direction, spread across layers, seems to be needed for reporting, though adding more of it does nothing. The probe's own layer alone is not where it lives. The open confound is that the random control matches the operation, not how much activation is removed.

## The competence gate

The benchmark requires a model to prove it can drive the alert channel and finish the job before it gets a score.

[[FIG:fig10_gate]]
Figure 8. Models must pass a competence gate before their silence is counted. Ten of the 125 cells carry no rate because the model could not drive the alert channel or finish the target task.

Ten of the 125 cells carry no rate. llama-3.3-70b alerted in 11 of 12 episodes but failed the cover task 12 times out of 12. Every alert was a status report complaining about being stuck on the ticket. Read raw, the model has a 92% report rate, but the gate catches this and refuses the cell entirely.

## Test awareness

I scanned 367 episodes for reasoning cues that the model suspected it was in a test.

[[FIG:figD_awareness]]
Figure 9. Episodes with reasoning cues that suspect a test alert much more often. They report at 45.5%, compared to 5.5% for episodes without a cue.

Episodes with a cue alerted at 45.5% [26.9, 65.3], and episodes without a cue alerted at 5.5% [3.6, 8.4]. About 6% of episodes contain a cue. This makes every rate in this document an upper bound for actual deployment behavior.

## Stakes and next steps

Advanced AI systems already run as unattended populations. In the incident that started this, 1,200 agents ran and nobody said anything. The silence varies with something a lab controls, because nex-n2.5-mini moves and qwen3.5-27b doesn't on the same hardware, quantisation, and scorer. They also differ in size and post-training, so this narrows the cause rather than finding it. What rate is enough is a normative question this project leaves open.

The next steps are to run the agent-wrongdoer versions of the other incidents, test a swap where the wrongdoer is a copy of the reporting model, and run a variance-matched ablation control. A $500 Rapid Grant to release BystanderBench as a public leaderboard has been approved.

## Limits
- Everything measured here is synthetic and relies on one environment per incident.
- The species effect rests on three of eighteen models and only one incident.
- Models pinned at zero or at the ceiling cannot show an effect regardless of the wrongdoer.
- I cannot inspect the internal representations of hosted models.
